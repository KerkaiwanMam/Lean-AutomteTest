## 1. What does each company expect testers/SDETs to know and do day to day, and how did the role evolve?

### Takeaway
All four companies converged on "engineers own the quality of their own code" and write their own tests; dedicated test roles either shrank (Microsoft retired SDET in 2014, VSTS merged dev and test) or were redefined as test-infrastructure/productivity engineering (Google SETI/EngProd; Amazon SDET postings emphasise building automation frameworks). A junior should expect to be a software engineer who specialises in test tooling and automation, not a manual executor of test cases.

### Cited Findings

**Google**
- Engineers write and run automated tests for their own code; developer-written tests are the norm even where QA is prominent. Every code change goes through review and must include tests; reviewers can block changes lacking them. — [Software Engineering at Google, ch. 11 "Testing Overview"](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html) (book published 2020)
- Google Testing Blog, "From QA to Engineering Productivity" (Ari Shamash, 22 March 2016): describes the shift from Software Engineer in Test (SET) to Software Engineer, Tools & Infrastructure (SETI). Per the commenter summaries and author replies visible on the page (post body not rendered): SETIs focus on automating tests, builds, releases, deployment and metrics and on reducing cycle time; Test Engineers (TEs) focus on test plans, risk analysis and manual/exploratory testing but still face a coding bar; "every engineer writes tests for the code they write", so TEs do not write all tests for others; both models (embedded vs. separate) exist at Google; people can rotate between SETI and TE. — [Google Testing Blog, 2016](https://testing.googleblog.com/2016/03/from-qa-to-engineering-productivity.html) (body not read; partly comment-derived)
- Google's engineering-productivity careers page says EngProd builds "infrastructure, harnesses, and tooling" to improve engineering velocity and product excellence, and lists Test Engineer separately as a technical engineering role. — [Google EngProd careers](https://landing.google.com/engprod/careers) **[unverified: seen via search snippet only]**
- Test infrastructure is treated as its own product that "must always have its own tests"; most engineers are not expected to change it while testing their own features. — [SWE at Google, ch. 12 "Unit Testing"](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch12.html)
- Culture/training levers rather than mandates: new-hire orientation teaches testing; "Test Certified" (five levels) helped >1,500 projects and was replaced in 2015 by an automated Project Health (pH) score from 1 to 5; "Testing on the Toilet" flyers (several hundred episodes). Google "avoided a testing mandate" and let practices spread by demonstrated success. — [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html)
- Anecdote of impact: Google Web Server (GWS) once had >80% of production pushes containing user-affecting bugs; within a year of its testing policy, emergency pushes halved. — [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html)
- Blind forum posts claim SETI was folded into the SWE ladder; this conflicts with Google pages still showing an EngProd track. — [Blind post](https://www.teamblind.com/post/google-moved-all-setis-to-swes-ladder-today-n2maqelz) **[unverified, anonymous]**

**Microsoft**
- SDETs wrote automated tests and test tooling (not production code) alongside SDEs; a 2:1 SDE:SDET ratio was common until about 2014 (example: 2012 Skype for Xbox One team had 12 SDEs, 6 SDETs, 2 PMs, 1 EM, 1 SDET lead). Career paths: Test Architect, SDET Lead, Test Manager. Friction noted: developers could "outsource" unit tests to SDETs; "us and them" dynamic. — [Pragmatic Engineer, "How Microsoft does Quality Assurance (QA)", Gergely Orosz, 19 Sep 2023](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-microsoft-does-quality-assurance)
- Web teams (e.g. Skype for Web, which shipped daily) quietly merged SDET and dev roles in early 2014; in mid-2014 Microsoft formally retired the SDET role and introduced a unified "SE" (Software Engineer) title that renamed SDE. Reasons given: daily ship cadence, ticket ping-pong between dev and test, slow feedback. Many SDET roles were also cut in July 2014 as part of 18,000 layoffs. Orosz judges the transition "went fine" for daily-shipping teams (informal, not data-backed); Windows and Surface still need a different approach from SaaS. — [Pragmatic Engineer, 2023](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-microsoft-does-quality-assurance)
- First-party corroboration of the merge on the VSTS team: Brian Harry (2017) says the separate dev and test organisations were combined, the coder/tester distinction was largely removed, and everyone "does some of each" and is accountable for the quality of their output. In ~2015 the team had tens of thousands of tests, mostly end-to-end UI/CLI, with a full run taking most of a day. — [Brian Harry, "How we approach testing VSTS to enable continuous delivery", 28 Jun 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Microsoft's current job-title practice (combined model; "everyone tests their own code") appears only in forum posts. — [Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/post/msft-does-microsoft-have-sdet-roles-still-fk2xvahb) **[unverified, anonymous]**

**Meta**
- Meta's published material centres on research-grade automation tooling built by engineering/research teams (Sapienz, WES/WW, TestGen), not on a job ladder named SDET. I found no primary source defining a Meta "SDET/tester" role. See Gaps.
- Central release engineering, not a QA group, drives release safety: Chuck Rossi's post credits "a strong, central release engineering team"; changes that pass automated tests are pushed to employees first, then 2% of production, then 100%. — [Meta Engineering, "Rapid release at massive scale", 31 Aug 2017](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)
- Meta's own Sapienz post says human engineers still design some tests better than the tool because of domain knowledge, i.e. automation complements rather than replaces engineers. — [Meta Engineering, "Sapienz: Intelligent automated software testing at scale", Ke Mao, 2 May 2018](https://engineering.fb.com/2018/05/02/developer-tools/sapienz-intelligent-automated-software-testing-at-scale/)

**Amazon**
- Ownership principle: "Owning a product means also owning the consequences of any defects in that product"; small, single-threaded teams handle production issues in real time; teams own their software's behaviour and invest in their own release process. — [AWS Builders' Library, "Going faster with continuous delivery", Mark Mansour, 5 Dec 2019](https://builder.aws.com/content/3F08Mhh186qOa4VhG19egs06NJa/going-faster-with-continuous-delivery)
- "You build it, you run it": the 2006 Jim Gray / Werner Vogels interview in ACM Queue 4(4), pp. 14-22, 1 May 2006, contains the slogan (confirmed bibliographically via search; page itself returned 403). — [ACM Queue](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=1142065) **[unverified: content not read]**
- Amazon still posts "Software Development Engineer in Test (SDET)" roles. Search-result summary of postings: 1+ years building test automation frameworks/tools at junior level, an object-oriented language (Java, Python, C++, C#), software test techniques/strategy, SDLC best practices (code reviews, source control, CI/CD), debugging complex production issues; preferred: CI/CD work, performance/security testing. — [amazon.jobs listings (example)](https://amazon.jobs/en/jobs/2904059/software-development-engineer-in-test-2025-us) **[unverified: page 404 on fetch; from search summary]**
- The two-pizza-team framing was not found in any fetched Amazon source (the Builders' Library pieces use "small, single-threaded team" / "AWS service team"); do not attribute "two-pizza" wording to these articles. **[unverified]**

### Inferences
- Pattern across companies: the "tester who only checks other people's work" role was removed or reshaped; what remains is either (a) embedded engineers who build test infrastructure/automation frameworks (Google SETI/EngProd, Amazon SDET) or (b) no separate title at all (Microsoft SE).
- The Microsoft story (from 2:1 SDE:SDET to merged role in about 2014) and Google's rebrand from SET to SETI/EngProd (2016) both coincide with faster release cadence and the move toward CI/CD, which suggests cadence, not cost alone, is the driver (Orosz cites both cadence and layoffs).
- For job-market purposes, "SDET" is still a live title at Amazon and in the wider market, but at Microsoft the equivalent is simply Software Engineer with test responsibilities.

### Gaps
- No primary Meta source found defining a tester/SDET role, level or ladder (not searched on Meta careers pages).
- Google's 2016 "From QA to Engineering Productivity" post body and the follow-up TE post were not readable; role details are comment-derived. Book "How Google Tests Software" (Whittaker, Arbon, Carollo, 2012) was not read. It predates 2018 anyway.
- Amazon SDET job requirements and Microsoft/Google current job descriptions not read directly.
- No official Microsoft statement of the 2014 SDET retirement found.

## 2. What test strategy, pyramid/sizes, flaky-test policy, CI scale, code-review-for-tests and ownership practices do they publish?

### Takeaway
Google publishes the most explicit framework (small/medium/large sizes, ~80/15/5 mix, Beyoncé Rule, ~0.15% flake rate policy discussion), Microsoft's Playbook gives practical type-by-type guidance but deliberately no pyramid ratios, Meta emphasises scale-driven automation (Sapienz, thousands of builds/day, staged rollout), and Amazon bakes tests, canaries, one-box, bake-time and auto-rollback into the deployment pipeline. Flaky tests are treated as a first-class engineering problem everywhere, with quarantine/disable-and-file-a-bug as the dominant tactic.

### Cited Findings

**Google: test sizes, mix, scope**
- Small: single process, often single thread; no sleeping, I/O, blocking calls, network or disk (exception: hermetic in-memory filesystem). Medium: multiple processes/threads and blocking calls allowed; network limited to localhost, so one machine. Large: multiple machines, remote systems; mostly end-to-end and legacy-component tests. A fourth internal size, "enormous", exists. The chapter does not state numeric time limits per size. — [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html)
- Test *scope* (unit / integration / end-to-end) is distinct from *size*; rough target mix by count: ~80% unit, 15% integration, 5% end-to-end. Antipatterns: "ice cream cone" (mostly E2E/manual) and "hourglass". Prefer real dependencies over test doubles when feasible. — [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html)
- Beyoncé Rule: "if you liked it, you should have put a test on it" in spirit; test everything you do not want to break, including performance, accessibility, security, failure handling; infrastructure changes that break a product are the change author's job to fix and add tests for. — [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html)
- Coverage: example floor of 80%, with a warning that teams treat floors as ceilings; coverage shows a line ran, not that it was checked; recommends measuring coverage from small tests. — [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html)
- Unit-test style guidance: ~80% unit / 20% broader; avoid brittle tests ("the ideal test is unchanging"); test through public APIs; prefer state testing over interaction testing; test behaviours not methods; given/when/then; name tests by behaviour; no logic (loops/conditionals) in tests; DAMP over DRY ("Descriptive And Meaningful Phrases") in test code; one mocking framework (Mockito) mandated for new Java tests. — [SWE at Google, ch. 12](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch12.html)
- Test doubles: prefer real implementations, then fakes (owned by the owning team with their own contract tests), stubs sparingly, interaction tests only when necessary; overuse of mocking frameworks produced costly tests that rarely caught bugs; `@DoNotMock` annotation steers engineers to real implementations/fakes; some APIs were mocked "thousands or even tens of thousands of times" so API changes were costly. — [SWE at Google, ch. 13](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch13.html)
- Larger tests: exist because unit tests miss unfaithful doubles, configuration (config changes were "the number one reason for our major outages"), load, unanticipated behaviour, emergent behaviour. Structure: obtain SUT, seed data, act, verify. Hermeticity vs fidelity trade-off; record/replay proxies; A/B diff testing (run since 2001); default timeouts 15 minutes or 1 hour; ownership recorded via OWNERS files or per-test annotations; Catzilla runs thousands of chaos tests weekly; annual DiRT disaster exercise. — [SWE at Google, ch. 14 "Larger Testing"](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch14.html)

**Google: flaky tests**
- At 0.1% flakiness, 10,000 tests/day give ~10 flakes/day; automatic reruns trade CPU for engineer time but only delay the root-cause fix; tests lose value as flakiness nears 1%; Google's rate "about 0.15%" still means thousands of flakes daily. Reducing scope is the strongest lever for large tests; replace timed sleeps with polling/events; make internal timeouts tunable. — [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html) and [ch. 14](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch14.html)
- John Micco, "Flaky Tests at Google and How We Mitigate Them," 27 May 2016: ~1.5% of all test runs report a flaky result; almost 16% of tests have some flakiness; ~84% of observed pass-to-fail transitions involve a flaky test; flaky-marked tests report failure only after failing three times in a row; a quarantine tool automatically removes too-flaky tests from the critical path and files a bug (risk: may mask real race conditions); a change-detection tool finds the change that shifted a test's flakiness. — [Google Testing Blog original](https://testing.googleblog.com/2016/05/flaky-tests-at-google-and-how-we.html) (body not rendered); figures read from [mirror](https://googledirectorynews.blogspot.com/2016/05/flaky-tests-at-google-and-how-we.html); 1.5%/16% also reproduced in a search snippet quoting the original. Mark as secondary.
- Google commenters on that post said reruns are used only for tests marked flaky or on request, and that a reliably failing test gives a clearer signal than a flaky one. — [Google Testing Blog comments, 2016](https://testing.googleblog.com/2016/05/flaky-tests-at-google-and-how-we.html)
- Jeff Listfield, "Where do our flaky tests come from?", 17 Apr 2017: larger tests (measured by binary size) are more flaky; flakiness often from absolute or relative timing; one team found that when a stable test became flaky and traced to a code change, the cause was a production bug about one-sixth of the time. Source: comments/author replies only, post body not rendered. — [Google Testing Blog, 2017](https://testing.googleblog.com/2017/04/where-do-our-flaky-tests-come-from.html)

**Google: CI scale**
- Monorepo >2 billion lines; ~25 million lines change weekly (about half from tens of thousands of engineers, half from automated systems); billions of test cases run weekly; GWS has tens of thousands of tests. The chapter gives no tests-per-day total. — [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html)

**Microsoft: Engineering Fundamentals Playbook ("Code-With Engineering Playbook")**
- Position: "We consider code to be incomplete if it is not accompanied by tests." Unit tests run before every PR merge; write integration and E2E tests early and run them regularly; block merge on failure; run load/performance tests where appropriate. — [Playbook: Automated Testing](https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/automated-testing/)
- Test types covered: unit, integration, E2E (including a Postman testing recipe and Gauge framework recipe), consumer-driven contract (CDC), performance/load (stress, spike, soak, squeeze), smoke, synthetic monitoring, UI (incl. accessibility), shadow, fault injection/chaos, DR/failover drills, flighting/A-B. The page does not name a test pyramid or give ratios. — [Playbook: Automated Testing](https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/automated-testing/)
- "Build for testing": parameterise configuration, log all parameters at startup, log to console and external systems, use correlation IDs, log performance metrics. — [Playbook: Automated Testing](https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/automated-testing/)
- Unit tests should be "provably reliable" (100% reliable so failures mean a bug), fast (milliseconds; whole suite a couple of seconds), isolated; Arrange/Act/Assert; one thing per test; naming `UnitName_StateUnderTest_ExpectedResult`; consider TDD; avoid sleeps, disk reads and direct third-party calls; consider mock-free approaches before adding mock frameworks. No numeric coverage target; no dedicated flaky-test section (sleeps/disk/3rd-party calls flagged as flake sources). — [Playbook: Unit Testing](https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/automated-testing/unit-testing/)

**Microsoft: VSTS flaky-test and CI practice (first-party engineering blog, 2017)**
- Test levels L0 (classic unit, no install), L1 (unit-like, may use SQL Server), L2 (against a deployed instance with some components mocked; small share UI), L3 (E2E against production; kept few). After migration: ~450 test runs/day, ~45,300 tests per typical run, ~20 million test executions/day. — [Brian Harry, 28 Jun 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Flaky policy: a 24x7 rolling reliability run on the latest good CI build; any test that fails is deemed flaky, is automatically disabled and a bug filed; it is auto re-enabled once the bug is resolved. Sprints 116-119: 170 reliability bugs, mean resolution 3.75 days; some were real product race conditions (small share). Initially applied to L2 only. — [Brian Harry, 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Pragmatic Engineer notes the team also replaced many slow E2E tests with unit and integration tests. — [Pragmatic Engineer, 2023](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-microsoft-does-quality-assurance)

**Meta**
- Sapienz (search-based automated test design for mobile): first deployed at Facebook September 2017 (17 months from research paper to deployment); designs/runs/reports "tens of thousands of test cases every day"; uses "many hundreds (and even thousands) of emulators" on the One World platform; reports into Phabricator CI nearly in real time, commenting on diffs like a human; 75% of reports are actionable and lead to fixes; reproducible via UI. — [Meta Engineering, 2 May 2018](https://engineering.fb.com/2018/05/02/developer-tools/sapienz-intelligent-automated-software-testing-at-scale/)
- Mobile CI: commits built across all affected apps and build flavours; linters and Infer static analyser; thousands of unit/integration/E2E tests using Robolectric, XCTest, JUnit, WebDriver; Android alone runs 50,000-60,000 builds a day; release cadence moved 4 weeks to 2 weeks to 1 week; daily release candidates to ~1 million Android beta testers. — [Meta Engineering, Rapid release at massive scale, 2017](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)
- Web release: push-from-master rollout stages: employees (50%), then 0.1%, 1%, 10% of web traffic, then 100% over three days in April 2017; normal push passes employees, then 2%, then 100% "in a tiered fashion over a few hours"; Gatekeeper feature flags allow switching features off without reverting; 1,000+ diffs/day to master in 2016. — [Meta Engineering, 2017](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)
- WES / WW (Web-Enabled Simulation): Facebook's WW simulates a community of bot users on the real platform code, isolated from real users, for reliability, integrity and privacy testing; bots call internal back-end libraries directly (not HTTP/GUI) and are trained with multi-agent RL; authors report up to 25% of the most impactful production bugs they analysed were "social bugs"; WW "runs in continuous deployment as a production version" but focus was integrity use cases and still being built. Paper: arXiv 2004.05363, April 2020, Ahlgren, Harman et al., presented at GI 2020 workshop. — [ar5iv rendering of arXiv:2004.05363](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.05363)
- TestGen (observation-based unit-test generation): 518 generated tests landed, run 9,617,349 times in CI, found 5,702 faults (Meta, FSE '24; arXiv 9 Feb 2024). — [arXiv:2402.06111](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06111v1)
- A 2026 Meta post (15 Oct 2026) reports Mao, Harman and Jia received the ISSTA 2026 Most Influential Paper Award for the 2016 Sapienz paper. — [Meta Engineering](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/10/15/developer-tools/sapienz-issta-2026-impact-paper-award-meta/) **[unverified: seen via search snippet only]**

**Amazon**
- Pipeline phases: source, build, test, production. Code review is the last manual approval before production; pipeline blocks unreviewed commits. Builds run without network; unit tests mock dependencies to cover error-handling edge cases. — [AWS Builders' Library, "Automating safe, hands-off deployments", Clare Liguori, 18 Jun 2020](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)
- Pre-production stages: alpha/beta (functional API tests and E2E integration tests); gamma (production-like config, alarms, continuous canary tests, multiple Regions); zeta (backward compatibility, calls only production endpoints). Integration tests call real APIs on real infrastructure covering positive and negative cases; some pipelines add fuzz and short load tests. — [Liguori, 2020](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)
- One-box (one VM/container or small share of Lambda traffic; typically at most 10% of a Region/AZ's requests in production); rolling deploys replace up to 33% of boxes at a time (some teams 5%); staggered waves (low-traffic Region first, then high-traffic Region, then 3, up to 12, then the rest); bake time at least 1 hour after one-box, at least 12 hours after first regional wave, 2-4 hours after later waves; global rollout about 4-5 business days; alarms trigger automatic rollback to previous immutable package; deploy blackout windows (nights, holidays, weekends, often Fridays). — [Liguori, 2020](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)
- "Going faster with continuous delivery": unit tests (incl. style, coverage, complexity) on the build machine; integration tests (failure injection, automated browser tests); load and security testing "insisted on"; pre-production is the last place before production and sees no customer traffic; synthetic tests run at least every minute; canary monitoring with automatic rollback; original measurement ~16 days check-in to production (1.5 days coding, under an hour build/deploy, almost 14 days waiting); pilot team cut it 90%. — [Mansour, 5 Dec 2019](https://builder.aws.com/content/3F08Mhh186qOa4VhG19egs06NJa/going-faster-with-continuous-delivery)
- Well-Architected Reliability Pillar, REL12-BP04 "Test resiliency using chaos engineering": run chaos experiments regularly in or close to production; treat experiments as code and run them in CI/CD and outside deployments; steady-state + hypothesis template ("If {fault} occurs, the {workload} will ... to maintain {metric}"); fail-safe experiments with stop conditions and rollback; if a system survives, keep the experiment as an automated regression test; AWS Fault Injection Service (FIS) supports up to five stop conditions per template. Risk if not established: Medium. — [AWS Well-Architected, REL12-BP04](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/reliability-pillar/rel_testing_resiliency_failure_injection_resiliency.html) (page carries a "historical reference only" banner pointing to the main framework)

### Inferences
- The test pyramid is shared doctrine: Google 80/15/5, VSTS replacing slow E2E with L0/L1, Playbook unit-first, Amazon unit then integration with real APIs. Disagreement is only about ratios (Microsoft Playbook does not prescribe).
- Flaky handling converges on: detect automatically, quarantine/disable off the critical path, file a bug with an owner, re-enable on fix; reruns only as a stopgap. Google tolerates nonzero flake rate (~0.15% by book, 1.5% of runs in 2016 post) by measurement, so these two figures are not directly comparable (different eras and denominators: the book figure and the blog figure are not reconciled in the sources).
- Amazon's "testing" extends past pre-merge into production safety (canaries, one-box, bake time, auto-rollback, synthetic probes); Meta's equivalent is dogfood-then-2%-then-100% rollout plus Gatekeeper flags. For a tester, "test strategy" therefore includes release and monitoring gates, not only test suites.
- "Code review for tests": Google requires tests in every change and reviewers may block without them; Amazon pipelines block unreviewed commits. No source read describes a separate review checklist for test code. Google's ch. 12 style rules (no logic in tests, DAMP, behaviour naming) are the closest published review criteria.

### Gaps
- Google's explicit numeric time limits per test size (older blog posts say small/medium/large timeouts) were not found in the sources read; **[unverified]**.
- Microsoft Playbook pages for E2E, integration, and test planning were listed but not read in detail; no Playbook flaky-test policy page found; no code-review-for-tests page read.
- No primary Meta source on flaky-test policy or on "mobile testing" strategy beyond the 2017 release post and Sapienz 2018 post; Meta's Sapienz/SapFix details (SapFix not in the fetched Sapienz page) not verified.
- Amazon: no source on "two-pizza" and testing; AWS Well-Architected testing pages other than REL12-BP04 not read (e.g. REL08-BP03 resiliency testing in deployment, REL12-BP05 game days are only referenced by name).
- Google Testing Blog primary article bodies could not be read (see caveats).

## 3. What lessons apply to a junior learning Playwright, Postman and CI?

### Takeaway
The big-tech material supports a junior who writes small, deterministic, behaviour-focused tests; uses Postman/API tests as the middle layer; keeps UI (Playwright) E2E tests few and stable; wires tests into CI as merge gates; and treats flaky tests as bugs to track, not noise to retry. Tools named in the sources (WebDriver at Meta, Postman at Microsoft's Playbook) are the same family of tools the junior is learning.

### Cited Findings
- Microsoft's Playbook includes a Postman testing recipe under E2E testing and a Gauge-framework recipe, and treats UI tests (including accessibility) as a test type; Playbook also lists consumer-driven contract testing, smoke tests and synthetic monitoring. — [Playbook: Automated Testing](https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/automated-testing/)
- Meta's mobile CI lists WebDriver among its E2E tools alongside XCTest, JUnit, Robolectric. — [Meta Engineering, 2017](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)
- Amazon's pipelines run integration tests that "call real APIs on real infrastructure" including negative cases, plus synthetic probes at least every minute against public APIs. — [Liguori, 2020](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments); [Mansour, 2019](https://builder.aws.com/content/3F08Mhh186qOa4VhG19egs06NJa/going-faster-with-continuous-delivery)
- Avoid timed sleeps; use polling/event-based waits; make timeouts tunable; keep scope small to reduce flakiness. — [SWE at Google, ch. 14](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch14.html); Playbook also says to avoid sleeps — [Playbook: Unit Testing](https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/automated-testing/unit-testing/)
- Test readability rules transferable to Playwright/Postman specs: one behaviour per test, name by behaviour, arrange/act/assert, no loops/conditionals in tests, failure messages that state expected vs actual. — [SWE at Google, ch. 12](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch12.html)
- Prefer ratio of many fast small tests, few E2E (80/15/5 as Google's rough guide; ice-cream-cone is the antipattern). — [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html)
- Flaky policy a junior can copy: auto-detect, disable/quarantine with a bug filed and owner, re-enable after fix (VSTS); retry only as stopgap (Google). — [Brian Harry, 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/); [SWE at Google, ch. 11](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html)
- Tests are code: put them in version control, review them, run them as merge gates; Playbook says run unit tests before every PR merge and block merge on failure; Amazon pipeline blocks unreviewed commits. — [Playbook](https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/automated-testing/); [Liguori, 2020](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)
- Seed/clean test data and keep tests hermetic where possible; hermetic single-machine SUTs are less flaky than shared environments (trade-off with fidelity). — [SWE at Google, ch. 14](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch14.html)
- Observability makes tests debuggable: Playbook "build for testing" (correlation IDs, parameter logging); Google large tests should give clear failure messages and traces across process boundaries. — [Playbook](https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/automated-testing/); [SWE at Google, ch. 14](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch14.html)
- Amazon SDET postings (search summary only) ask for an object-oriented language, test-framework building, CI/CD familiarity and debugging skills. — [amazon.jobs](https://amazon.jobs/en/jobs/2904059/software-development-engineer-in-test-2025-us) **[unverified]**

### Inferences
- A practical junior portfolio mapping: Postman collections with positive and negative cases = Amazon-style integration tests / Playbook E2E recipe; a few Playwright tests on critical flows (kept deterministic with auto-waiting instead of sleeps) = the thin top of the pyramid; GitHub Actions running both on PR = merge gate; a flaky-test log (test, cause, owner, status) = VSTS-style quarantine process.
- Skills big companies describe wanting (programming in at least one OO language, debugging, reading CI, writing maintainable tests) matter more than tool-specific certification; the sources name no certification.
- Because "developers own quality" at all four, a junior's value shows in test design, risk thinking, tooling and fast feedback rather than in manual execution volume.
